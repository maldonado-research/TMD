# Mutation supply, establishment and the next TMD test

**Targeted literature update; publication cutoff 1 October 2026, America/Los_Angeles.** These five studies were absent from the literature notes inspected in TMD's main repository and public research extensions 0.2–0.5. They are newly identified for this review, not necessarily newly published since the previous review. This is not a systematic review. Crossref, PubMed and available primary full text were checked; inspection limits are explicit below. Bibliographic and access details are in `SOURCES.json`.

The useful advance is a stronger set of competing explanations. Recent experiments distinguish particular mutation classes from overall mutation rate, connect mutation bias to the distribution of measured growth effects, and show that reproductive variation can evolve. These findings help design tests of the proposed successful-arrival approximation `lambda ∝ mu A F`. None tests TMD's common supply-adjusted Wsp/Aws/Mws contrast across contexts, and none supplies the required matched W/A/M observation and recovery-control ledger.

## Five sources and what they establish

**1. Adrita Chakraborty and Deepa Agashe, “Mutation bias influences the emergence and effects of antibiotic resistance.”** [bioRxiv preprint](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.18.745421), posted **21 August 2026**. No journal version was verified: the exact DOI record has no publication relation, and a targeted Crossref title search returned this preprint.

The registered abstract describes *E. coli* mutators exposed to antibiotics with different cellular targets. The authors report that specific mutation rates generally predicted high resistance better than overall mutation rate, and that recovered resistance mutations reflected the mutators' spectra. The outcome includes the highest concentration permitting overnight resistance emergence and resistance probability. This is a particularly direct reason to compare TMD against route-specific supply, rather than genome-wide mutation rate alone. Overnight emergence combines mutation production with survival, growth and detection; it cannot be relabeled an exact first-successful-arrival time.

**Inspection and data limit:** authoritative bibliographic metadata and the registered abstract were read. Direct bioRxiv full-text/API requests were blocked by the network proxy. Sample sizes, protocol details, raw-data links and recovery controls were not verified. No quantitative effect size is imported.

**2. Shazia Parveen, Aakanksha Madhwal, BK Ruchith, Mrudula Sane and Deepa Agashe, “Mutation bias is a key predictor of adaptation rate.”** [bioRxiv preprint](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.22.677663), posted **24 September 2025**. No journal version was verified by the DOI relation, targeted Crossref title search or exact-title PubMed search.

The registered abstract reports experimental evolution of wild-type and mutator *E. coli* in different growth media. Reversing ancestral mutation bias promoted early adaptation; mutation bias, mutation rate and initial fitness jointly predicted relative strain adaptation rates in the studied comparisons. This motivates a comparator using independently measured supply and starting fitness. It does not establish that one shared route adjustment predicts a new context. Nor do strain-level adaptation rates identify W/A/M winner probabilities.

**Inspection and data limit:** metadata and abstract only; bioRxiv full text was blocked. Sample units, prospective versus retrospective prediction, data availability and dependence on earlier experiments remain unverified. This paper and sources 1 and 3 share investigators; their independence must be checked before counting them as separate replications.

**3. Mrudula Sane, Shazia Parveen and Deepa Agashe, “Mutation bias alters the distribution of fitness effects of mutations.”** Published research article, *PLOS Biology* 23(7):e3003282, **14 July 2025**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3003282); [primary full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12273949/).

The Methods identify **694 isolates for single-step fitness comparisons** from wild type and six repair-mutant backgrounds, with growth effects measured in LB and glucose minimal medium. Mutation-accumulation experiments, whole-genome sequencing and growth assays were used to construct strain-specific distributions of fitness effects. The authors correct these distributions for selection during colony growth. Their results associate reversal of the ancestral transition bias with a larger beneficial fraction, with differences among strains and media.

For TMD, this makes a concrete alternative available: the mixture of mutations supplied can change the distribution of measured growth effects. Route aggregation can therefore hide variation in both supply and performance. The measured maximum exponential growth rate is a fitness proxy, not a direct establishment probability. Mutation accumulation reduces selection but does not eliminate it; the authors' correction and block structure should be retained in any reanalysis.

**Reproducibility:** relevant Methods, Results, Table 1 footnotes and the data statement were inspected in primary PMC XML. Supporting files identify S2 Data as per-line mutation counts/types and S3 Data as raw LB/glucose fitness data; S1–S7 Scripts describe mutation-processing steps. The article declares CC BY. The XLSX files were not acquired by this literature review: attempted direct PMC attachment URLs returned 404. No counts were extracted from those files. Some lines reuse earlier work. Table 1 explicitly states that 80 wild-type mutations came from 38 founding lines; 46 represent later mutational steps whose fitness was measured against the immediate ancestor. The 694 comparisons must therefore not be treated as 694 independent founding-line trials.

**4. Joao A. Ascensao, QinQin Yu and Oskar Hallatschek, “The evolution of genetic drift over 50,000 generations.”** Published research article, *Genetics*, **3 August 2026** online. [Journal DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyag199); [earlier preprint full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12873942/), posted **27 January 2026**, DOI [10.64898/2026.01.25.701616](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.01.25.701616).

The journal abstract reports 33 clones from two LTEE populations, combining neutral barcode trajectories with census measurements and Bayesian inference. Descendant-number variance changed during evolution and diverged between populations. The preprint's Methods explicitly model sequencing noise separately from latent lineage frequencies; its Discussion treats the physiological cause of the variance change as unresolved.

This supplies a measurable establishment-side alternative to treating population size or mean growth as sufficient. Its approximate relationship between establishment and descendant variance is model dependent, not a universal route formula. A variance effect shared equally by all routes can change the arrival clock while canceling from route proportions; explaining a route-contrast change requires relevant route or background interactions.

**Reproducibility and version limit:** journal metadata and abstract were verified; detailed Methods and Discussion were read in the earlier preprint, not the journal full text. The [repository README](https://github.com/joaoascensao/genetic-drift-evolution) identifies experiment metadata, colony counts, barcode trajectories, inference outputs and raw reads at BioProject PRJNA1129587. A versioned [Zenodo deposit](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21431098), published 18 July 2026, identifies the `Genetics` archive and CC BY 4.0 license. Deposit metadata and README were read; the approximately 1.49-GB archive was not downloaded or executed. Barcode reads and inferred frequencies are not independent introduced-cell recovery trials.

**5. Elisa Heinrich-Mora and Marcus W. Feldman, “Evolution under Stochastic Transmission: Mutation-rate Modifiers.”** Published research article, *Theoretical Population Biology* 168, **21 February 2026** online; April 2026 issue. [Journal DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tpb.2026.02.003); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41730461/). Crossref explicitly links the **4 November 2025** [preprint](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.11.04.686407) to this journal article.

The journal abstract describes a two-locus mutation-modifier model in which mutation rates vary across generations. Invasion depends on their temporal distribution, selection and recombination, rather than mean mutation rates alone. It is a theoretical counterexample to treating a mean transmission parameter as generally sufficient. It neither demonstrates such dynamics in W/A/M cultures nor establishes a mechanism for TMD. Journal abstract and metadata were inspected; journal full text and any code were not inspected. There is no experimental recovery ledger in the evidence inspected.

## Consequence for the empirical plan

Prespecify a comparison using context-specific, route-specific mutation supply; independently measured growth or establishment information; the restrictive TMD contrast; and a flexible context/background alternative. Keep published growth-rate, endpoint resistance, inferred drift and first-arrival observations tied to their actual measurement models. Examine supply uncertainty, background dependence and recovery separately.

No source is admitted to the current matched W/A/M likelihood. The strongest newly identified reusable data opportunity is a separate audit of the Sane growth-effect data or the versioned Ascensao drift archive. Either could improve biological baselines and study design; neither can substitute for matched W/A/M supply, selected outcomes, exact fixed-route recovery denominators and a justified control-transfer model. This review provides no independent validation of TMD and no claim of a breakthrough.
